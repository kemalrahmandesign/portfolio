Osmosis

01

No playbook to copy


the fun part

When I joined, there was no settled way to design a decentralized exchange. Banking apps stand on decades of patterns. Crypto had a few years, most of them spent showing raw protocol mechanics straight to the user.

Osmosis runs as its own blockchain and moves assets between dozens of others. Most of the problems on my desk had never been solved by anyone, let alone written up. So the job was designing systems, not screens: how a swap explains a route that hops through pools, what a transaction says while it waits on a chain, what failure even looks like when the network is the backend.

I was one of the first designers working on these problems. There was nothing to benchmark against, only traders, and what they told us. The design team was two people: my boss, the lead designer, and me.

Why it’s hard

Designing for crypto is designing for complexity.

  • Every asset behaves differently
  • Every chain has its own speed and quirks
  • A swap can route through pools you never see
  • Transactions fail, stall, or land at a different price
  • Fees are paid in tokens that change by the minute
  • Nothing can be undone

Every interaction carried dozens of states. The job was never to hide that system. It was to build an interface that could hold all of it and still feel calm.

02

Listening first


a lot of calls

Before touching a screen, we talked to the people trading on it: experienced DeFi traders, liquidity providers, cross-chain users inside Cosmos, and people arriving from centralized exchanges.

I logged every interview in Airtable and Notion, tagged so patterns surfaced across conversations instead of living in one person’s head. The same four things kept coming up:

  • Trades took too many steps
  • Market data was hard to scan
  • Complexity with no structure
  • Pros wanted speed

Pools, perps, margin, liquid staking: everything a trader moves between, one ecosystem.

03

The trade screen


where it all happens

The trade screen is the heart of Osmosis. It is where people read the market and act, often with seconds to spare, so it had to be readable under pressure.

Buy, sell and swap live in one card. Pick a size with a tap, and the quote fills in both sides in tokens and dollars. Underneath sit the details traders kept asking for: the rate, the fee, and the route.

The route is the part crypto usually hides. A BTC to OSMO swap might hop through pools, each with its own fee. Instead of a wall of pool IDs, it reads as a line you can follow, token to token, with the cost of each hop on it.

Everything they need, nothing in the way.

A real swap: tap 50%, read the quote, open the route.

04

One-click trading


fewer pop-ups

Serious traders swap fast and often, and classic DeFi makes them approve every single step in a wallet pop-up. One-click trading replaces that with a single approval up front.

The hard part was staying honest: faster should never mean blind. So turning it on is one toggle, with the two limits that matter set right there: a loss protection limit and a session period. One signature starts the session, and from then on the app tells you it is active and how long it has left, in the toast and in your profile.

One approval, then just trade.

Turning it on: toggle, set the limits, one signature. The session counts down in your profile.

05

Charts and pools


making money mechanics friendly

Traders live on visual signals. I designed charts and market indicators that surface price movement, trading activity and performance trends, and stay scannable when the market goes wild.

Liquidity pools are how Osmosis works at all: people supply assets and earn fees from trades. They are also genuinely confusing. The pool screens translate that into plain terms: how a pool is built, how your position changes over time, and how trading affects what you earn.
